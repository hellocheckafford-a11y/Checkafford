How to Calculate What Mortgage You Can Afford

Mortgage affordability is the largest monthly payment you can carry without your debts taking too big a share of your income. Lenders work it out from four numbers: your gross monthly income (before taxes), your other monthly debt payments, your down payment, and the full housing cost: principal, interest, property taxes, homeowners insurance, HOA dues and mortgage insurance. This calculator runs the same two ratios a lender checks. If you want a starting price range by salary first, see How Much House Can I Afford on My Salary?

The 28/36 rule: the US lending standard

The housing ratio (front-end ratio) divides your full monthly housing cost by your gross monthly income. The common guideline is 28% or less.

The total debt-to-income ratio (back-end DTI) adds car payments, student loans, credit card minimums and other loans to the housing cost. The guideline is 36% or less. Many lenders accept up to about 43%, and some programs, such as FHA loans, can go to about 50% with strong compensating factors like savings or a high credit score. The calculator shows both ratios and flags each threshold.

What really drives your mortgage payment

Three factors move your payment the most: the loan amount, the interest rate, and the term. A 30-year term lowers the monthly payment, but at about 7% the total interest comes to roughly 1.4 times the amount you borrow.

At rates between 6% and 7%, a 15-year mortgage cuts total interest by more than half, but the monthly payment is roughly 35–45% higher. Use the calculator to find the right balance for your income.

Worked example: the calculator's default numbers

Take a gross income of $8,000 a month, $1,200 in other debt payments, a $400,000 home with $80,000 down, a 7.1% rate over 30 years, and about $600 a month for taxes and insurance:

  1. Loan amount: $400,000 − $80,000 = $320,000.
  2. Principal and interest: about $2,151 a month. Adding $600 for taxes and insurance gives a housing cost of about $2,751.
  3. Housing ratio: $2,751 ÷ $8,000 = 34.4%, above the 28% guideline.
  4. Total DTI: ($2,751 + $1,200) ÷ $8,000 = 49.4%, above the roughly 43% many lenders accept.

Same income, same $80,000 down, but a $320,000 home: the loan falls to $240,000, principal and interest to about $1,613, and with roughly $500 in taxes and insurance the housing ratio drops to 26.4%. Total DTI is still 41.4%, so the bigger lever here is the other debt. Cutting it from $1,200 to $700 a month brings total DTI to about 35%, inside the 36% guideline.

Common questions

What is the difference between the housing ratio and total DTI?
The housing ratio counts only your housing cost: principal, interest, property taxes, insurance, HOA dues and mortgage insurance. Total DTI adds every other monthly debt payment. Lenders look at both, but total DTI usually decides the approval, because it is the ratio with a hard limit in most loan programs.
What counts as monthly debt in the DTI calculation?
Recurring payments that show up on your credit report or are legally required: car loans, student loans, personal loans, the minimum payment on each credit card, and child support or alimony. Utilities, groceries, phone bills, car insurance and the rent you will stop paying once you buy are not counted.
Why does the calculator use gross income instead of take-home pay?
Because US lenders qualify borrowers on gross income, before taxes and payroll deductions. That makes the ratios look smaller than what you actually feel each month. Check the Left Over figure too: it is your gross income minus debts and housing, so taxes, retirement contributions and living costs still have to come out of it.
Is a $2,500 mortgage payment too much on a $70k salary?
On $70,000 a year (about $5,833 a month gross), a $2,500 housing payment is a 43% housing ratio, far above the 28% guideline. Since total DTI also includes your other debts, it would be at or above the roughly 43% many lenders accept even if you owed nothing else. Most lenders would see it as a stretch, and it leaves little room for savings or emergencies.
Do I need 20% down to get a mortgage?
No. Some conventional loans allow 3% down and FHA loans allow 3.5% down. With less than 20% down on a conventional loan you will usually pay private mortgage insurance (PMI), often around 0.5% to 1.5% of the loan amount per year. FHA loans charge their own mortgage insurance (MIP) instead. Add either one to the monthly costs field so your ratios are accurate.
Should I include property taxes and insurance?
Yes. Lenders qualify you on the full monthly housing payment, often called PITI (principal, interest, taxes and insurance), plus HOA dues and mortgage insurance. Leaving them out can make an unaffordable home look fine: in the worked example above, $600 a month of taxes and insurance adds 7.5 points to both ratios.
