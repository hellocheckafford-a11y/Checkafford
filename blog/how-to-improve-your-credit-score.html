How to Improve Your Credit Score in 90 Days

Your credit score affects your interest rates, loan approvals, and even your rent application. Here is what actually moves the needle — and what does not.

By Elba Nabetcy Guissepe Avalo, actuary
Updated October 2026
4 min read

Why Your Credit Score Matters More Than You Think

Most people only think about their credit score when they need a loan. By then it is too late to improve it. Your score quietly affects your interest rate on every loan, whether you get approved for an apartment, and how much you pay for car insurance.

The difference between a 620 and a 750 score on a $25,000 car loan can be over $4,000 in extra interest. On a mortgage, that difference can be $50,000 or more.

What Makes Up Your Credit Score

Payment history (35%) is the largest factor. Credit utilization (30%) is second. Length of credit history (15%). Credit mix (10%). New credit inquiries (10%).

Fastest Wins: What Moves in 30 Days

Two things move your score in 30 days: paying down credit card balances and disputing errors on your credit report.

  • Pay down credit card balances — keeping utilization below 30% (lower is better) usually helps your score, and the change can show up within a billing cycle or two
  • Dispute errors — a 2012 FTC study found that one in five consumers had an error on at least one of their credit reports. Check free at AnnualCreditReport.com
  • Ask for a credit limit increase — if spending stays the same but limit goes up, utilization drops
  • Become an authorized user on a family member's card with good credit

Medium-Term Moves: 90 Days

Pay every bill on time for 90 days straight. Set up autopay for the minimum on every account. One missed payment can drop your score 50 to 100 points and stays on your report for 7 years.

What NOT to Do

  • Do not close old credit cards — shortens your history and increases utilization
  • Do not apply for multiple new cards at once — each drops your score 5 to 10 points
  • Do not max out a new card right after opening it
  • Do not pay a credit repair company — everything they do legally, you can do free

Realistic Timeline

If your score is in the 500s, you can reach 650 to 680 in 6 to 12 months. If in the 600s, hitting 720 is achievable in 12 to 18 months with consistent effort.

How Credit Utilization Works

Utilization is your card balances divided by your credit limits. If you owe $4,500 on cards with $10,000 in total limits, your utilization is 45%. Paying that down to $3,000 brings it to 30%, and getting under $1,000 puts you below 10%, which tends to score best. Issuers usually report your balance around the statement closing date, so paying before the statement closes can lower the number that gets reported.

Check Your Reports Before You Fix Anything

You can get free reports from Equifax, Experian, and TransUnion every week at AnnualCreditReport.com. Look for accounts you do not recognize, wrong balances, and late payments that were actually on time. If you find an error, dispute it online with the bureau that shows it and keep copies of everything. Bureaus generally have 30 days to investigate.

Hard Inquiries vs. Soft Inquiries

A hard inquiry happens when you apply for credit, and it usually costs a few points. Hard inquiries stay on your report for two years but affect your score mainly in the first year. Checking your own score is a soft inquiry and has no effect. When you shop for a mortgage, auto loan, or student loan, multiple inquiries within a short window (14 to 45 days, depending on the scoring model) are typically counted as one, so compare lenders within a few weeks.

Building Credit From Scratch or After Damage

  • Secured credit card: you put down a deposit that becomes your limit, then use it lightly and pay in full
  • Credit-builder loan from a credit union: the payments are held in savings for you and reported as on-time history
  • Authorized user: ask someone with a long history of on-time payments and low balances to add you, and confirm the issuer reports to the bureaus
  • Rent and utility reporting: some services add these on-time payments to your file, but check which bureaus and scoring models use them

How Long Negative Items Stay

Late payments and collection accounts generally stay on your report for seven years, and a Chapter 7 bankruptcy for up to ten. Their impact fades as they age, and recent good behavior counts for more every month. That is why a plan of steady on-time payments works even when the damage looks bad.
