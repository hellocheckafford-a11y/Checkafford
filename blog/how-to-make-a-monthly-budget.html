How to Make a Monthly Budget from Scratch

Most people skip budgeting because it feels complicated. It is not. Here is a simple system that works even if you have never budgeted before.

By Elba Nabetcy Guissepe Avalo, actuary
Updated October 2026
4 min read

Why Most People Avoid Budgeting

Budgeting has a reputation problem. People think it means tracking every penny or giving up everything fun. None of that is true. A budget is simply a plan for your money — telling it where to go before it disappears.

Without a budget, most people spend reactively. They pay bills, buy what they want, and hope something is left at the end of the month. A budget flips that: you decide in advance what matters most, and fund those things first.

Step 1 — Know Your Real Monthly Income

Start with your take-home pay — the amount that hits your bank account after taxes, not your gross salary. If your income varies, use your lowest month from the past 3 months as your baseline.

Step 2 — List Every Fixed Expense

Fixed expenses are the same every month: rent, car payment, insurance, loans, subscriptions. Write them all down with exact amounts. These happen whether you budget or not — knowing the total is the foundation.

Example Fixed Expenses

Rent $1,200 · Car $380 · Insurance $145 · Phone $65 · Subscriptions $45 — Total: $1,835/month

Step 3 — Estimate Variable Expenses

Variable expenses change month to month: groceries, gas, dining out, entertainment. Look at your bank statements from the last 3 months and calculate an average. Most people underestimate food and entertainment.

Step 4 — Apply the 50/30/20 Rule

50% of take-home goes to needs (housing, food, transport, utilities, minimum debt payments). 30% goes to wants (dining out, entertainment, hobbies). 20% goes to savings and extra debt payments.

50/30/20 Example — $4,000 Take-Home

Needs (50%): $2,000 — rent, groceries, car, insurance · Wants (30%): $1,200 — restaurants, streaming · Savings/Debt (20%): $800 — emergency fund, extra loan payments

Step 5 — Track and Adjust Every Month

Review your budget every Sunday for 10 minutes. Compare planned versus actual spending. Adjust next month accordingly. After 3 months you will have a budget that fits your life.

The goal is not perfection — it is awareness. When you know where your money goes, you can make intentional choices about changing it.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Forgetting irregular expenses like car maintenance — divide annual costs by 12 and include monthly
  • Setting unrealistic targets — gradual reductions work better than drastic cuts
  • Not having a miscellaneous category — budget $50 to $100 per month for unexpected small expenses
  • Giving up after one bad month — budgets take 3 months to dial in

Pick a Method That Fits You

  • 50/30/20: simple and flexible, good if you want a quick framework
  • Zero-based: give every dollar a job until income minus plan equals zero
  • Envelopes: set a cash or digital limit for categories where you overspend
  • Pay yourself first: automate savings on payday, then spend what is left

A Full Example: $4,000 Take-Home

CategoryMonthly amount
Fixed expenses (rent, car, insurance, phone, subscriptions)$1,835
Groceries$450
Gas$160
Utilities$190
Dining out and fun$300
Personal and miscellaneous$100
Sinking funds$165
Savings and extra debt payments$800
Total$4,000

Notice that this budget is not 50/30/20. Needs (rent, car, insurance, phone, groceries, gas, utilities) come to $2,590, or about 65%. Wants add up to $445 (about 11%), and savings plus sinking funds reach $965 (about 24%). That is perfectly fine. The percentages are a starting point, and in high-cost areas needs often take more than half. What matters is that every dollar has a plan and savings is funded first.

Sinking Funds for Irregular Expenses

Irregular bills break budgets because they feel like surprises. A sinking fund turns them into small monthly amounts. In the example above, the $165 could be split like this:

  • Car maintenance and repairs: $60
  • Holidays and gifts: $40
  • Medical copays and prescriptions: $35
  • Annual fees and renewals: $30

If Your Income Changes Month to Month

Build the budget on your lowest recent month. In better months, fill your needs first, then your savings, and park any extra in a buffer account. Over time, that buffer lets you pay yourself a steady amount, like a salary, even when the paychecks are uneven.

Choose Tools You Will Actually Use

A spreadsheet, a notes app, your bank's built-in tracking, or a paper notebook all work. The best tool is the one you open every week. Start simple and add detail only when the habit is solid.

When the Numbers Do Not Add Up

If your needs are bigger than your income, trimming coffee will not fix it. Look at the largest lines first: housing, transportation, and debt payments.
