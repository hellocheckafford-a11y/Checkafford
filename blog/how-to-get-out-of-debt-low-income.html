How to Get Out of Debt on a Low Income

Getting out of debt when money is tight feels impossible. It is not — but it requires a different strategy than what most financial advice assumes.

By Elba Nabetcy Guissepe Avalo, actuary
Updated October 2026
4 min read

Why Standard Debt Advice Fails Low-Income Households

Most debt payoff advice assumes you have hundreds of extra dollars each month. Cut your lattes, cancel Netflix, and you have $500 extra. This advice is useless if your budget is already stripped to the bone.

Getting out of debt on a low income requires maximizing every dollar, finding income you did not know existed, and using strategies designed for tight budgets.

Step 1 — Stop Adding to Your Debt

Before paying off debt, stop adding to it. No new credit card charges except for true emergencies. If you cannot stop using cards, freeze them literally in water in the freezer. The friction stops impulse purchases.

Step 2 — Negotiate Your Interest Rates

Call your credit card company and ask for a lower rate. It works more than you think — especially if you have been a customer for a while. A drop from 24% to 18% APR on $5,000 saves $300 per year.

  • Call and ask to speak to retention
  • Mention you have received offers from other cards with lower rates
  • Ask specifically for a temporary or permanent rate reduction
  • If they say no, ask about hardship programs

Step 3 — Find Every Dollar

On a low income, accelerating debt payoff means finding money in your current situation.

  • Sell items you no longer use — Facebook Marketplace and OfferUp work quickly
  • Check if you qualify for benefits not being used — SNAP, utility assistance, free healthcare
  • File your taxes — many low-income households leave the Earned Income Tax Credit unclaimed
  • Ask your employer about overtime or extra shifts

Step 4 — Use the Debt Snowball

List debts from smallest to largest balance and put every extra dollar toward the smallest while paying minimums on the rest. Each eliminated debt reduces your minimum payment burden.

Why the Snowball Works on Low Incomes

Eliminating a $150/month minimum payment frees up $150 every month going forward — a cascade effect without extra income.

What to Do in a True Crisis

If you genuinely cannot make minimum payments, contact creditors before missing payments. Most have hardship programs that temporarily reduce or suspend payments. Communicating gives you options.

Get Free Help Before You Pay Anyone

Nonprofit credit counseling agencies, including members of the National Foundation for Credit Counseling (nfcc.org), offer free or low-cost help with budgeting and debt. A counselor can review your situation and may set up a debt management plan, where you make one monthly payment and the agency negotiates lower interest rates with your creditors. Be careful with companies that charge large upfront fees or tell you to stop paying your creditors. Under FTC rules, debt relief companies that sell by phone cannot charge fees before they settle a debt.

Know Your Rights With Debt Collectors

  • You can ask for written validation of the debt within 30 days of the collector's first contact
  • Collectors cannot harass you, threaten you, or call at unreasonable hours, generally before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m.
  • Before paying an old debt, check how old it is: in some states, a payment can restart the time limit for suing
  • Keep every letter and write down dates and names for each call

Lower Your Other Bills First

Every dollar you do not need to spend on basics is a dollar for debt. Calling 211 connects you with local help for rent, utilities, and food. Many utilities have hardship programs, and the federal LIHEAP program helps eligible households with energy bills. If you have medical debt, ask the provider for an itemized bill, and ask about financial assistance, since nonprofit hospitals are required to have such policies. If you have federal student loans, check studentaid.gov for the repayment plans available to you, because the options have been changing.

Free Tax Help and the EITC

The Earned Income Tax Credit goes unclaimed by about one in five eligible workers. The IRS also runs the Volunteer Income Tax Assistance (VITA) program, which prepares returns for free for people with lower incomes, generally those earning about $67,000 or less (the limit is updated each year). A refund can be a big, one-time boost for your first debt.

A Small Snowball in Action

Say you owe $400 on Card A (minimum $25), $1,100 on Card B (minimum $35), and $1,500 on Card C (minimum $45). The minimums total $105. Add just $25 extra and keep the full $130 going every month. Before interest, Card A is gone in 8 months. Its $50 then rolls into Card B, which is gone around month 18. Then the whole $130 goes to Card C, and you are debt-free in about two years. Interest will add some time, but the pattern holds: the payment stays the same while the debts disappear.

Protect Yourself From Quick Fixes

Payday loans, title loans, and cash advances can carry triple-digit APRs and turn a short gap into a long cycle. If you are tempted, first ask your employer about a paycheck advance, check with a local credit union about small-dollar loans, or call 211.
