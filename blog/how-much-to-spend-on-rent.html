How much should I spend on rent?

The old rule says 30% of income on rent. In many American cities in 2026, that rule is hard to follow. Here is what actually works.

CheckAfford
Updated October 2026
4 min read

The 30% rule comes from U.S. public housing rules: a 25% cap set in 1969 and raised to 30% in 1981. The problem: it was designed for a housing market that no longer exists. Nationally, about half of U.S. renters spend more than 30% of their income on rent, and the share is higher in cities like New York or Miami.

The Better Rule: 50/30/20

Financial experts increasingly recommend the 50/30/20 budget: 50% of after-tax income for needs (rent, food, utilities, minimum debt payments) · 30% for wants · 20% for savings and debt payoff.

Under this framework, rent can exceed 30% of gross income — as long as total essential expenses stay under 50% of net income.

When High Rent Is Genuinely a Problem

  • You have zero savings after paying rent each month
  • You are using credit cards to cover groceries or utilities
  • You cannot contribute to retirement — even the employer match
  • An unexpected expense of $500 would cause a crisis

Three Ways to Find Your Limit

  • 30% of gross income: the classic benchmark. At $60,000 a year that is $1,500 a month.
  • The 3x rule: many landlords ask for a gross monthly income of at least three times the rent, which works out to about 33%.
  • 50/30/20 on take-home pay: total needs, including rent, food, utilities, and minimum debt payments, should stay near 50% of what lands in your bank account.

Example: $60,000 a Year

How the Three Methods Compare

Gross monthly income: $5,000 · 30% rule: $1,500 · 3x rule: about $1,667 · 50/30/20 with about $4,100 of take-home pay (it varies by state and deductions): $2,050 for all needs. If food, utilities, transportation, and minimum debt payments take $1,100, only $950 is left for rent.

The 30% rule tells you what you can usually get approved for. The 50/30/20 method shows what you can live with. When your other needs are high, the real limit can be well below 30%.

Rent Is Not the Whole Housing Cost

Before you sign, add the costs that come with the apartment: electricity and gas, internet, water and trash if they are not included, parking, pet fees, renters insurance, and application or admin fees. Together they can easily add $150 or more a month, and they should fit inside your budget before you compare apartments. Ask the landlord which utilities are included.

Plan for Rent Increases

Rent often rises at renewal. Ask what increase to expect and keep a buffer: if a 5% increase would push your rent above your limit, treat the higher number as the real price. On a $1,500 rent, 5% is $75 more per month, or $900 more per year.

How to Lower Your Housing Costs

  • Add a roommate, or choose a place with an extra room you can share
  • Look at neighborhoods a short commute away, since rent often drops quickly with distance
  • Ask about discounts for longer leases or for paying early
  • Negotiate at renewal, especially if the building has vacancies
  • Compare total cost, including utilities, rather than the rent line alone

A simple final test: after rent and your other fixed costs, you should still be able to save something every month. If you cannot, the rent is too high for your situation even if it is under 30%.
