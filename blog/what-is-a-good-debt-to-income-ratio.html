What is a good debt-to-income ratio?

Your debt-to-income ratio is the single number lenders care about most. Here is what it means, what is considered good, and how to improve it.

By Elba Nabetcy Guissepe Avalo, actuary
Updated October 2026
4 min read

When you apply for any loan, lenders look at dozens of factors. But one number carries more weight than almost anything else: your debt-to-income ratio (DTI).

What Is Debt-to-Income Ratio?

The Formula

DTI = (Total Monthly Debt Payments ÷ Gross Monthly Income) × 100 · Example: $1,500 in debts ÷ $5,000 income = 30% DTI

5 Ways to Lower Your DTI

  • Pay off small debts first — eliminating a $150/month payment drops your DTI immediately
  • Avoid new debt before applying — every new loan raises your DTI
  • Increase your income — a side job raises the denominator in the DTI equation
  • Pay down credit card balances — lower balances mean lower minimum payments
  • Refinance high-payment loans — extending a term lowers the monthly payment and your DTI

Front-End vs. Back-End DTI

Lenders actually look at two versions of this number. The front-end ratio counts only your housing costs: mortgage principal and interest, property taxes, homeowners insurance, and HOA dues. The back-end ratio adds every other monthly debt payment on top. When people say "DTI", they usually mean the back-end ratio, because it shows the full picture.

Example — $6,000 Gross Monthly Income

Housing: $1,680 ÷ $6,000 = 28% front-end · Housing + car loan ($300) + student loan ($150) = $2,130 ÷ $6,000 = 35.5% back-end

What Counts as Debt and What Does Not

DTI uses the minimum monthly payment on debts that show up on your credit report or in loan documents. It does not include everyday living costs.

  • Usually counted: the mortgage or rent payment you are applying for, car loans, student loans, personal loans, minimum credit card payments, child support and alimony
  • Usually not counted: groceries, utilities, phone, car insurance, health insurance, streaming and other subscriptions
  • Gray area: Buy Now Pay Later plans and deferred student loans. Some lenders count them, so ask before you apply.

How Much DTI Do Lenders Allow?

There is no single national limit. Each loan type has its own guidelines, and each lender can add stricter rules of its own. As a rough guide, most lenders prefer a back-end DTI at or below about 36% to 43%. Some programs approve ratios in the mid-40s or even higher when other parts of your application are strong, such as a high credit score, a large down payment, or several months of savings. Approvals above 50% are uncommon and usually depend on special programs or strong compensating factors.

Treat those numbers as a range, not a promise. The only way to know your limit is to ask the specific lender and loan program you are considering.

DTI Is Not Your Credit Score

Your DTI does not appear on your credit report, and it does not change your credit score. A person with an excellent score can still be declined for a high DTI, and a person with a modest score and very little debt can qualify easily. Lenders check both, because the score shows how you have handled debt in the past and the DTI shows whether you can afford more of it today.

Real Example: Lowering DTI From 46% to 37%

Applicant earning $5,000 per month

Starting point: new housing payment $1,210 + car $520 + student loans $310 + credit card minimums $260 = $2,300 ÷ $5,000 = 46% · Pay off the credit card with savings: $2,040 ÷ $5,000 = 40.8% · Add $500 per month of side income: $2,040 ÷ $5,500 = 37.1%

Removing a payment lowers the top of the fraction, and adding income raises the bottom. Both moves count, and either one alone can be enough to move you into a better lending range.
