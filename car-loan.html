    How to Calculate How Much Car You Can Afford

    To know if you can afford a car, look past the sticker price and the monthly payment. What matters is the amount you finance (price minus down payment and trade-in, plus sales tax and fees if you roll them in), the APR, the loan term, and what the car costs to run: insurance, fuel and maintenance. This calculator checks those numbers against the 20/4/10 rule and your debt-to-income ratio. For a starting budget by salary, see How Much Car Can I Afford on My Salary?

    The 20/4/10 rule

    A common guideline for car buyers: put at least 20% down, finance for 4 years (48 months) or less, and keep the loan payment plus insurance under 10% of your gross monthly income.

    The down payment protects you from owing more than the car is worth, the short term limits interest, and the 10% cap leaves room for the rest of your budget. The calculator checks each part separately, so you can see which one to fix.

    What DTI means for your car loan

    Debt-to-income (DTI) is the share of your gross monthly income that goes to debt payments. For an auto loan, lenders add your rent or mortgage, credit card minimums, student loans, other loans and the new car payment, then divide by your income.

    Many lenders look for 43% or less with the new payment included, though limits vary by lender, credit score and down payment. The calculator flags a DTI above 43% as high risk.

    Worked example: the calculator's default numbers

    A $32,000 car with $4,000 down, 7% sales tax, $600 in fees rolled into the loan, 7.5% APR for 72 months, $170 a month of insurance, a $6,000 gross monthly income and $400 in other debt payments:

    1. Amount financed: $32,000 − $4,000 down + $2,240 sales tax (7%) + $600 fees = $30,840.
    2. Payment at 7.5% APR over 72 months: about $533. With $170 of insurance, that is $703, or 11.7% of $6,000.
    3. The 20/4/10 check: 12.5% down (misses 20%), 72 months (misses 4 years), 11.7% of income (misses 10%).
    4. Debt-to-income: ($400 + $533) ÷ $6,000 = 15.6%, well within what lenders accept.

    So the loan is easy to get approved but misses all three parts of the rule. With fuel ($160) and maintenance ($90), owning the car costs about $953 a month. To pass all three with the same income and rate, the payment has to stay near $430, which at 48 months is a loan of about $17,800: roughly a $19,700 car with $3,950 down.

    Common questions

    Is 72 months too long for a car loan?
    It lowers the payment but costs more and keeps you owing more than the car is worth for longer. With the calculator's default numbers ($30,840 financed at 7.5%), 72 months means about $533 a month and $7,552 in interest; 48 months means about $746 a month and $4,953 in interest, roughly $2,600 less. If only the 72-month payment fits your budget, the car is probably too expensive for now.
    What counts toward the 10% part of the 20/4/10 rule?
    This calculator uses the most common version: the loan payment plus insurance, compared with your gross monthly income. Stricter versions also count fuel and maintenance, which is why the results also show the monthly cost to own the car. If that full figure is well above 10% of your income, the car will feel more expensive than the payment suggests.
    Should I roll sales tax and fees into the loan?
    Only if you cannot pay them up front. In the default example, $2,240 of sales tax and $600 of fees are financed, which adds about $695 of interest over 72 months and leaves you owing more than the car is worth from day one. Uncheck the option in the calculator to see the cash due at signing instead.
    What happens if I owe more on my trade-in than it is worth?
    That gap is called negative equity, and dealers usually add it to the new loan. You then pay interest on debt from a car you no longer own, and the new loan starts underwater. Enter the trade-in value and what you still owe, and the calculator adds the difference to the amount financed.
    What interest rate can I expect with my credit score?
    Rates depend heavily on credit. In Experian's Q2 2026 data, the average new-car APR was about 6.4% overall, about 4.4% for super-prime borrowers (781–850) and about 13.5% for subprime borrowers (501–600). Used-car loans run several points higher in every tier. Get a preapproval from a bank or credit union before visiting the dealer and enter that APR here.
    Will a car loan affect my chances of getting a mortgage?
    Yes. The car payment counts in your debt-to-income ratio for years. With the default numbers, a $533 payment on a $6,000 monthly income uses almost 9 points of DTI, which reduces how much house a lender will approve. If you plan to buy a home soon, run both numbers together in the mortgage calculator.
