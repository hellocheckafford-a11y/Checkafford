How to Calculate If a Personal Loan Is Worth It

A personal loan is affordable when the payment fits your budget and the total cost is worth what the money does for you. Four numbers decide it: the amount, the interest rate, the origination fee and the term. The fee matters more than most people expect, because it usually comes out of the money you receive while you still repay the full amount. This calculator shows the cash you actually get, the real APR including the fee, your debt-to-income ratio, and whether the loan beats keeping the debt you already have. If you are choosing between a loan and a card, see Personal Loan vs Credit Card: Which Is Better?

When a personal loan makes sense

The strongest case is debt consolidation: replacing high-interest credit card balances with one fixed payment at a clearly lower real APR and a set end date.

They can also make sense for a necessary one-time expense, like an urgent home or car repair, when you have no savings to cover it and the payment fits your budget.

When to avoid a personal loan

If the loan pushes your debt-to-income ratio above about 43%, many lenders will decline it or charge a much higher rate, and even 36–43% leaves little margin if your income drops.

Avoid them for vacations or shopping, and be careful with consolidation if the new term is much longer than your current payoff time: a lower payment can still mean more total interest.

Worked example: the calculator's default numbers

A $10,000 loan at 11.5% for 36 months with a 5% origination fee, used to pay off a $9,500 credit card balance at 24% APR that you pay $300 a month:

  1. Cash you receive: $10,000 − 5% fee ($500) = $9,500, enough to pay off the $9,500 balance.
  2. Loan payment: about $330 a month for 36 months, $30 more than you pay now.
  3. Total cost of the loan: about $2,371 in interest and fee. Real APR with the fee: about 15.1%, still well below 24%.
  4. Keeping the current debt at $300 a month: 51 months and about $5,700 in interest.

The loan saves about $3,329 and finishes 15 months sooner, and debt-to-income on a $4,500 income moves only from 15.6% to 16.2%. The fee is the weak spot: the same loan with no fee would cost about $1,871, so an offer with no origination fee at a slightly higher rate can still be the better deal.

Common questions

What is an origination fee and how does it change the real APR?
It is a one-time charge, usually taken out of the money you receive. With the calculator's defaults, a $10,000 loan at 11.5% with a 5% fee pays out $9,500, but you repay it as if you had received $10,000: about $330 a month for 36 months. Measured against the cash you actually get, the real APR is about 15.1%, so the fee adds about 3.6 points. Compare offers by APR, not by the interest rate.
How much should I borrow if the lender deducts a fee?
Divide the amount you need by one minus the fee. To get $10,000 in hand with a 5% fee, you would borrow about $10,530, which raises the payment to about $347 a month. The calculator shows this figure under the results whenever you enter a fee.
Does consolidating credit card debt with a personal loan always save money?
No. It saves money when the real APR of the loan is clearly lower than your cards and you keep the cards paid off afterward. It can cost more if the new term is much longer than your current payoff time, if the fee is high, or if the card balances build up again. Enter your current balance, APR and payment, and the calculator compares both paths side by side.
What interest rate can I expect on a personal loan?
It depends mostly on your credit score and income. Personal loan APRs commonly run from about 6% to 36%. Bankrate's June 2026 average was about 12.3% for a borrower with a 700 FICO score, a $5,000 loan and a 3-year term. Borrowers with excellent credit can get single-digit rates, while fair or poor credit usually means 20% or more. Prequalify with several lenders, which uses a soft credit check, and enter the best real APR here.
Does a personal loan hurt my credit score?
Prequalifying does not, because it uses a soft inquiry. The formal application adds a hard inquiry, which usually lowers your score a few points for a short time. If you use the loan to pay off credit cards, your credit utilization drops, which often helps your score, and on-time payments build positive history.
How much can I borrow with a personal loan?
Many lenders offer between about $1,000 and $50,000, and some go higher. The limit depends on your income, credit and existing debts. Lenders usually want your debt-to-income ratio, including the new payment and your rent or mortgage, at 36% or less, and some approve up to about 43–50%.
