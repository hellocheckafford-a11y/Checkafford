    How Much Room Your Debt-to-Income Ratio Leaves

    Your debt-to-income ratio (DTI) is your total monthly debt payments divided by your gross monthly income, before taxes. Lenders look at it in two ways: the housing ratio, with only your rent or mortgage, and the total ratio, with every required debt payment. Knowing the percentage is only half the answer; the other half is how many dollars you have left before the limits lenders use. This calculator shows both ratios, your room in dollars before 36% and 43%, and the loan that fits in that room. For the formula step by step, see How to Calculate Your Debt-to-Income Ratio.

    Front-end vs. back-end ratio

    The housing (front-end) ratio counts only your rent or mortgage payment, including property tax, insurance and HOA. The common guideline is 28% of gross income.

    The total (back-end) ratio adds every other required debt payment: car, student loans, card minimums, personal loans, BNPL and support. The guideline is 36%, and many lenders cap it near 43%.

    Why the room in dollars matters

    A percentage tells you where you stand; the dollar room tells you what you can still take on. If you have $500 of room before 36%, a new $600 car payment would push you over, whatever the dealer approves.

    The calculator turns that room into a loan amount using the APR and term you enter, so you know the size of loan that fits before you apply.

    Worked example: the calculator's default numbers

    A gross income of $6,000 a month with rent of $1,650, a $420 car payment, $250 in student loans and $120 in card minimums:

    1. Housing ratio: $1,650 ÷ $6,000 = 27.5%, just under the 28% guideline.
    2. Total payments: $1,650 + $420 + $250 + $120 = $2,440.
    3. Total DTI: $2,440 ÷ $6,000 = 40.7%, above 36% but under 43%.
    4. Room before 36% ($2,160): none, $280 over. Room before 43% ($2,580): $140.

    At 8% APR over 60 months, $140 a month supports a loan of only about $6,900, and it would put this person at the 43% limit. Paying off the car loan changes the picture: total DTI drops to 33.7%, with $140 of room before 36%.

    Common questions

    What counts as a debt payment?
    Required monthly payments on loans and credit: mortgage or rent, car loans, student loans (even if deferred, lenders often count a payment), credit card minimums, personal loans, BNPL plans and court-ordered support. Utilities, phone, groceries, insurance you pay separately and subscriptions do not count.
    Does rent count in debt-to-income?
    For a mortgage application, lenders replace your rent with the new mortgage payment. For a car or personal loan, many lenders look at your rent or housing payment along with your debts. This calculator shows both ratios so you can see each view.
    What is a good debt-to-income ratio?
    As a rule of thumb, 36% or less is comfortable, 36% to 43% is tight, and above 43% many lenders decline or charge more, although some loan programs go to about 50% with strong credit or savings. For the housing part alone, the common guideline is 28%. See What Is a Good Debt-to-Income Ratio? for more detail.
    Should I use gross or take-home income?
    Use gross income, before taxes and payroll deductions, because that is what lenders use. If you are self-employed, lenders generally use your net business income after expenses, usually averaged from your recent tax returns, so use that figure instead of your revenue.
    How can I lower my DTI quickly?
    Paying off a debt removes its whole payment, so the fastest wins are debts with a high payment and a small remaining balance. Lowering a balance without ending the loan usually does not change the payment, except on credit cards, where the minimum falls with the balance. Raising your income also lowers the ratio, and avoiding new credit keeps it from rising.
    Does my debt-to-income ratio affect my credit score?
    No. Credit reports do not include your income, so DTI is not part of your score. Lenders calculate it separately when you apply. What does affect your score is credit card utilization, the share of your card limits you are using, which is a different ratio.
